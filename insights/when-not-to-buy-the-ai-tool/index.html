Why we sometimes tell a client not to buy the AI tool

Some of the most valuable lines in an audit start with "don't". Five signals the tool won't pay yet, and a payback test you can run on a napkin.

Andrija Popović 25 September 20265 min read

Every audit ends with a ranked list of options. Some of the most valuable lines on that list start with "don't".

That's not an anti-technology position. It's arithmetic. A tool that costs more to run, supervise, and fix than the delay it removes is a bad investment, no matter how impressive the demo was. Here are the signals we look for.

1. The volume is too low to matter

If a task happens 15 times a month and takes 10 minutes, that's 2.5 hours a month. Even at a generous loaded cost, automating it is unlikely to beat the subscription and the setup time. Low-volume tasks are often better solved with a template and a named owner.

2. The variation is the work

Some tasks look repetitive but aren't. Pricing a non-standard job, handling an upset customer, deciding which technician goes where when two emergencies land at once - the judgment is the value. Automating it moves risk without removing work.

3. The data isn't there yet

AI drafting a quote from free-text technician notes will produce free-text quality. When the input is messy, the first project is usually structure: a form, a template, required fields. The AI step comes after, and is often much smaller than originally planned.

4. An error costs more than the delay

An AI phone agent that saves 30 seconds per call but misroutes 1 emergency in 200 may be a very expensive saving. We always price the downside next to the upside, including the cost of customers who never call back.

5. You already own the fix

Field-service software, CRMs, and shared inboxes ship with reminders, routing rules, templates, and automations that most teams never switch on. Before buying anything new, we check what's already paid for. It's the cheapest win in most audits.

A payback test you can run on a napkin

1. Annual cost of the leak, as a range. Hours lost times loaded cost, plus gross profit lost to slow or missed follow-up. Low, base, and high.

2. First-year cost of the fix. License, setup, integration, staff training time, and someone's time to monitor it every week.

3. Compare. If the base case doesn't pay back within about 12 months, or the low case never pays back, wait - or find a cheaper fix.

This is deliberately rough. Its job is to stop bad projects early, not to prove good ones. The audit does the careful version: interviews, your own data, validated assumptions, and a pilot with a rollback plan.

What "don't" usually turns into

Almost never "do nothing". Usually it's a smaller, cheaper, faster step: a named owner for follow-up, a 3-touch cadence, a structured form, a feature switched on. Those can start working within weeks. The AI project, if it's still worth doing, comes later on cleaner data.
