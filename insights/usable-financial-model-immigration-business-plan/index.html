What a usable financial model looks like in an immigration business plan

You don't need to be a financial analyst to test a model. Seven checks, about 10 minutes, and you'll know whether the numbers will be easy for counsel to check.

Andrija Popović 25 September 20266 min read

A financial model that nobody can trace is a liability. It looks precise, it fills pages with tables, and the first time someone asks "where does this number come from?" it has no answer.

Counsel doesn't need to rebuild a model to judge it. These are the checks we run on our own work before it leaves the building - and the ones we run first when we audit someone else's.

1. There is an assumptions register you can read without opening a formula

Every number that drives the model - price, volume, growth, wages, rent, cost of goods - should sit in one list, in plain language, with a source and a date. "Average hourly rate for HVAC technicians in Harris County, BLS OEWS, May 2025" is an assumption. "Wages: $55,000" is not.

If the register doesn't exist, the model can't be reviewed. It can only be believed.

2. Revenue is built from units, not percentages

A credible revenue line is a multiplication: price times volume, limited by capacity. How many customers, jobs, or units; at what price; with how many people or trucks or seats to deliver them.

"Revenue grows 20% a year" is a red flag on its own. Growth should come out of the model - more staff, a second location, a new service - not be typed into it.

3. Staffing ties to the org chart, exactly

Open the org chart and the payroll schedule side by side. Same titles, same headcount, same years. Payroll in the P&L should equal wage times headcount for each role, with the wage source in the assumptions register.

It is an easy inconsistency to miss, because the org chart and the model are often built by different people at different times.

4. The investment is used, visibly

For E-2 and EB-5 matters especially, the use-of-funds table should add up to the investment amount, and each line should appear in the model: equipment in capital expenditure, build-out in the cash-flow timeline, working capital in the opening balance. An investment that sits in a table and never touches the model is a story, not a plan.

5. There is a cash-flow statement, not just a P&L

A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash in month 7. A usable model shows monthly or quarterly cash for at least the first year, and the month the business covers its own costs.

6. Someone asked "what if we're wrong?"

A plan that only works in the base case invites questions. Look for a simple sensitivity: what happens to profit, cash, and hiring if revenue comes in 20% lower, or costs 10% higher? The point isn't pessimism. It's showing the plan survives reasonable doubt.

7. You received the editable file

Counsel and the client should be able to change a number and see what moves. A PDF of a model is a picture of a model. If the numbers change before filing - and they often do - an editable file is the difference between a 10-minute update and a rebuild.

The 10-minute check

  • Find the assumptions register. Pick 3 assumptions and look for a source and date.
  • Find the revenue build. Is it price times volume, or a growth rate?
  • Compare Year 1 and Year 5 headcount in the model and the org chart.
  • Add up the use-of-funds table. Does it match the investment amount?
  • Find the cash-flow statement and the break-even month.
  • Look for one sensitivity case.
  • Confirm you have the editable file.

If more than 2 of these fail, the model will likely need work before counsel relies on it - and it's cheaper to find that now than in an RFE.

This is how we build every model: an assumptions register, a unit-based revenue build, staffing that ties to the org chart, and an editable file with the handover. See our documentation services, or ask us to audit a model someone else built.
