How to put a number on a slow quote follow-up

You can't fix what you can't price. A worked example with one formula, one hour of data gathering, and a range instead of a guess.

Andrija Popović 25 September 20266 min read

Most owners know their quote follow-up is patchy. Few can say what it costs. And without a number, it's hard to decide whether it deserves a process change, a tool, or nothing at all.

Here is the method we use, simplified so you can run it on your own data in about an hour.

The formula

Gross profit at risk per year = quotes with no follow-up × extra close rate from following up × gross profit per job

Three inputs. Two you can measure today; one you estimate as a range and then test.

Step 1: Count the quotes nobody chased

Export your quote list for the last 90 days from your job software or spreadsheet. For each open or lost quote, check whether anyone contacted the customer again within 7 days of sending it. Count the ones where nobody did, then scale to a year.

Example: 120 quotes in 90 days, so about 480 a year. 35% had no second touch: about 168 quotes a year.

Step 2: Find gross profit per job

Use gross profit, not revenue. Revenue overstates what you lose, because a lost job also saves its materials and labor. Average job value times your gross margin is close enough.

Example: $3,750 average job at 40% gross margin, so $1,500 gross profit per job.

Step 3: Estimate the close-rate lift as a range

This is the input you can't measure yet: of the quotes nobody chased, how many more would have closed with a follow-up? Don't pick one number. Pick a cautious, a middle, and an optimistic one.

Example: 3%, 6%, and 10%.

Put it together

CaseUnchased quotesExtra close rateGross profit per jobAt risk per year
Low1683%$1,500$7,560
Base1686%$1,500$15,120
High16810%$1,500$25,200

Illustrative numbers. Use your own.

Now you have a decision frame. If the low case alone pays for a simple fix - a named owner and a reminder cadence costs little more than attention - do it this week. If only the high case justifies a new tool, test before you buy.

Step 4: Test the lift cheaply

The range exists because the lift is a guess. Replace the guess with evidence: for 30 days, follow up every quote sent on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, and leave the others as they are today. Compare close rates. It's not a lab experiment, but it's far better than a vendor's case study.

What this method leaves out, on purpose

  • Staff time. Hours spent re-typing or chasing are a separate number - capacity, not lost profit. Keep them apart so you don't double count.
  • Lifetime value. A customer won today may buy again. Counting that makes the number bigger and harder to defend. Start without it.
  • Slow first replies. Leads lost before a quote is ever sent are a different leak with a different formula.
